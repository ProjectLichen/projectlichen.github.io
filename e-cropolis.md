# E-Cropolis

![E-Cropolis — Cities That Grow](From%20SimCity%20to%20E-Cropolis.png)

> **A city learning to become a living system.**

E-Cropolis is an experimental Project Lichen extension of the ideas embodied in SimCity and its open-source descendant, Micropolis.

The city remains.

People still need housing.

Businesses still operate.

Roads, infrastructure, energy, taxation and economic activity still matter.

But another question is introduced:

> **How much living capability can an existing city recover without ceasing to be a city?**

E-Cropolis does not begin by replacing the city with an idealized ecological settlement.

It begins with the city we already recognize and asks what might happen if living capabilities progressively became part of its functioning.

---

## From SimCity to E-Cropolis

SimCity demonstrated something remarkable.

Relatively simple interacting rules could produce recognizable urban behavior without scripting the final city in advance.

Population, land value, traffic, pollution, taxation, development and infrastructure interacted.

The player intervened.

The simulation responded.

Micropolis preserved this lineage as open-source software, making the underlying simulation available for inspection, experimentation and extension.

E-Cropolis proposes another step:

**SimCity → Micropolis → observable simulation → living-system extension**

The objective is not to simulate an answer.

> **Don't simulate the answer.  
> Simulate the relationships from which answers can emerge.**

---

## Micropolis Learning to Grow Food

The first E-Cropolis experiment can be deliberately small.

> **What is the smallest change we could make to Micropolis that would allow a crop to exist?**

One crop.

One place to grow it.

One growing cycle.

**seed → plant → harvest → food + seed**

Even this simple addition introduces relationships that conventional city simulation rarely needs to consider.

Where does the seed come from?

Does the crop require water?

What happens to soil?

How long does growth take?

How much food is produced?

Is reproductive seed retained?

What happens if all of it is consumed?

What happens during drought?

Does the next crop depend upon imports?

A single crop begins connecting the city to biology.

## A City Has More Than Economic Capability

Conventional city simulations understandably emphasize economic and infrastructural systems.

E-Cropolis retains those systems while adding another layer.

A city may possess capabilities involving:

**food**

**seed**

**soil**

**water**

**energy**

**biodiversity**

**materials and biological cycles**

**knowledge**

**community**

**governance and participation**

These are not intended simply to become additional resource counters.

The interesting part is the relationships among them.

Food production may depend upon soil, water, seed, energy, knowledge and labor.

Soil capability may depend upon organic material cycles.

Pollinators may depend upon habitat.

Seed capability depends upon successful reproduction and retention.

Human capability may depend upon all of them.

---

## Buildings Can Become Living Infrastructure

A building does not have to be represented as either built infrastructure or living system.

It may participate in both.

A residential tower might:

**house people**

**collect rainwater**

**generate electricity**

**support vines**

**provide pollinator habitat**

**compost biological residues**

**produce food**

**retain seed**

A roof can become productive space.

A wall can become habitat.

A street can influence water movement.

A vacant lot can become soil-building space.

The physical city does not disappear.

Its relationships become richer.

---

## From Resources to Cycles

E-Cropolis is particularly interested in moving beyond isolated inventories.

Instead of simply counting food, water or waste, we can begin observing cycles.

For example:

**soil → plant → food → people → organic residues → soil**

or:

**seed → crop → harvest → food + seed → next crop**

or:

**rain → capture → use → infiltration → living system**

What is commonly called waste may sometimes be material that has fallen out of a useful cycle.

This suggests another question:

> **Can the city become better at keeping useful materials in productive relationships?**

## What Capabilities Remain?

A city can achieve an immediate objective while reducing its future ability to achieve that objective again.

Food can be consumed without retaining seed.

Production can increase while soil deteriorates.

Economic activity can grow while dependence upon distant food, water, energy or material systems increases.

Efficiency can increase while redundancy disappears.

E-Cropolis therefore introduces the central Project Lichen question into the simulation:

> **What capabilities remain after the city has done what it does?**

Can it produce food again?

Can seed reproduce?

Can soil continue functioning?

Can water continue cycling?

Can energy remain available?

Can knowledge be retained?

Can communities adapt?

Can damaged capabilities regenerate?

And perhaps most importantly:

> **Have any of those capabilities been enlarged?**

---

## Financial Value and Living Capability

Cities operate through financial systems.

E-Cropolis does not remove them.

Money, taxation, prices, investment, property values and economic activity remain useful representations of human coordination.

But financial representation and underlying capability are not necessarily the same thing.

Two cities might possess identical financial wealth while having very different abilities to produce food, retain water, generate energy, reproduce seed or respond to disruption.

E-Cropolis may allow us to observe those differences.

The question is not whether money is good or bad.

It is:

> **How closely does the representation of value correspond to the capabilities upon which life actually depends?**

---

## Distance and Dependency

Cities depend upon relationships extending far beyond their boundaries.

Food travels.

Energy travels.

Materials travel.

Information travels.

Money and representations of value travel.

E-Cropolis may allow these dependencies to become visible.

A city importing nearly all of its food may function perfectly well under normal conditions while possessing little local food-producing capability.

The same may be true of energy, seed, knowledge, technology or materials.

Distance is not automatically undesirable.

The useful question is:

> **What capability exists locally, what depends upon elsewhere, and what happens when those relationships change?**

This connects E-Cropolis with Project Lichen's evolving inquiry into [Technology](technology.md) and Data Distance.

## Simulation, Artificial Intelligence and Human Judgment

E-Cropolis distinguishes between simulation and interpretation.

The underlying simulation should determine what happens according to its rules.

Artificial intelligence can observe what happened.

It may identify patterns, compare histories, propose explanations, ask questions or suggest experiments.

Humans decide what matters and what to try next.

In simple terms:

**Simulation:** What happened?

**Interface:** Show me what happened.

**AI:** What might it mean?

**Human:** What shall we try next?

This separation matters.

Artificial intelligence should not quietly manufacture the physical outcome that it later claims to have discovered.

The simulated world should remain available for inspection and repeated experimentation.

## Alternate Futures

One possibility becomes particularly interesting once a deterministic simulation can be observed by different decision-making systems.

Identical cities could begin from identical conditions.

One might be managed entirely by a human player.

Another might receive suggestions from a local AI system.

Another might use a different AI model.

Another might experiment with different governance or economic structures.

After ten, fifty or one hundred simulated years we could compare them.

Not merely:

**Which city became richest?**

or:

**Which city grew fastest?**

but:

**Which capabilities survived?**

**Which became dependent upon distant systems?**

**Which multiplied?**

**Which disappeared?**

**What possibilities remain available to each city?**

The objective would not be to discover a universally optimal city.

It would be to make consequences observable.

---

## Governance and Coordination

E-Cropolis may eventually provide a laboratory for experimenting with different ways of coordinating human activity.

These might include conventional governance, cooperative structures, DAO-based coordination, points systems, tokens or other mechanisms for representing contribution and exchange.

Again, the objective is not to assume that one mechanism is inherently superior.

The simulation allows us to ask:

**What behavior does this system encourage?**

**What does it measure?**

**What does it overlook?**

**Where does capability accumulate?**

**Where does dependency accumulate?**

**What happens over time?**

## The Commons

Some capabilities may behave differently from conventional resources.

Knowledge can spread without disappearing from its source.

Seeds can multiply through growing and sharing.

Open software can be copied, inspected and adapted.

Ecological knowledge can accumulate across participants and generations.

E-Cropolis may eventually allow us to explore how commons emerge, persist or fail within a simulated city.

As Project Lichen asks elsewhere:

> **A commons is not merely something we share.  
> It is something we collectively keep capable.**

See [The Commons](commons.md).

## An Experiment, Not a Prescription

E-Cropolis is not intended to present a blueprint for the perfect city.

We do not know what such a city would look like.

That uncertainty is useful.

The experiment instead asks whether relationships among biological, technological, economic and human systems can be made visible enough that consequences emerge from their interaction.

Some ideas will work.

Some will fail.

Some assumptions will probably prove wrong.

Unexpected behavior may be more interesting than expected behavior.

That is precisely why simulation is useful.

## The Technical Experiment

The initial technical path begins with the open-source Micropolis simulation.

The emerging architecture separates several responsibilities:

**Micropolis**  
Determines the simulated world's state and consequences.

**Interface**  
Makes relevant state observable and allows interventions.

**AI observer**  
Examines histories, identifies patterns, forms hypotheses and proposes experiments.

**Human participant**  
Chooses values, questions, interventions and what should be tried next.

The intention is to preserve a crucial distinction:

> **Simulation determines what happens.  
> AI can help us understand what happened and what might be tried.  
> Humans decide what matters and what to do next.**

## Where We Begin

Despite the size of the questions, the first experiment need not be large.

We can begin with:

**one crop**

**one growing space**

**one biological cycle**

and ask whether Micropolis can learn something it previously did not need to know:

> **A seed is not merely a resource.  
> It is the possibility of another harvest.**

From there, relationships can be added only when the experiment requires them.

Soil.

Water.

Organic material.

Pollinators.

Energy.

People.

Economics.

Governance.

Knowledge.

Not all at once.

One relationship at a time.

---

## The Question

E-Cropolis may eventually contain many systems, measures and experiments.

But underneath them is a remarkably simple question:

> **What happens when a city is evaluated not only by what it produces, but by what it remains capable of producing, reproducing, adapting to and becoming?**

That question connects E-Cropolis directly to the larger Project Lichen inquiry.

> **Run the city.  
> Watch what happens.  
> Ask what capabilities remain.**
