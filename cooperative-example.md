#From the Loom to the Cooperative#

Project Lichen's interest in cooperative federation did not begin with DAOs.
Nearly two centuries before blockchains, distributed networks or digital governance, communities in the industrial towns of northern England were confronting a surprisingly familiar problem:
How can people retain local agency while combining their resources to create capabilities they cannot achieve alone?
The answer that emerged around Rochdale would eventually influence cooperative movements throughout the world.
But it arose from a particular landscape.
The Landscape of the Loom
During the nineteenth century, the towns of Lancashire became part of an immense industrial system.
Cotton grown thousands of miles away crossed oceans to Liverpool and travelled into the mills and weaving sheds of towns such as Burnley.
Inside those mills, machinery transformed the cotton into cloth at extraordinary scale.
The machinery was so loud that ordinary conversation could become impossible.
Workers developed their own means of communication. In Lancashire weaving sheds, exaggerated lip movements and gestures—sometimes called mee-mawing—allowed people to communicate across the cacophony of machinery.
Human beings adapted themselves to the industrial system around them.
But adaptation did not necessarily mean that the system served them well.
The same industrial economy that created extraordinary productive capability also produced dangerous working environments, insecure livelihoods, pollution and dependence upon distant supply chains.
The Lancashire Cotton Famine of the 1860s revealed just how fragile that apparent abundance could be.
When supplies of American cotton were disrupted during the American Civil War, mills thousands of miles away slowed or stopped.
Machinery remained.
Factories remained.
Workers remained.
Yet much of their productive capability disappeared because one distant connection in the system had been broken.
It is an early illustration of a question Project Lichen continues to ask:
Does possessing infrastructure mean that a community possesses capability?

Twenty Miles Away
Only a relatively short journey from Burnley, the industrial town of Rochdale was confronting many of the same pressures.
In 1844, twenty-eight working people established the Rochdale Society of Equitable Pioneers.
More than half of the group were associated with the textile trades, including a substantial number of weavers. Their circumstances were therefore intimately connected with the same industrial transformation occurring across Lancashire. Real Rochdale
They began modestly.
By pooling their limited resources, the members opened a small shop at 31 Toad Lane, providing basic foods at dependable quality and fair measure. The enterprise was owned and governed by its members rather than by an external proprietor. The Rochdale Pioneers
The significance of Rochdale was not simply that working people had opened a shop.
They had begun constructing an alternative relationship between:
ownership, consumption, capital, governance and community.
People who possessed little economic power individually discovered that cooperation could create capabilities none possessed alone.
From Cooperation to Federation
The next development may be even more relevant to Project Lichen.
Individual cooperative societies did not have to merge into one enormous organization in order to increase their capabilities.
They could cooperate with one another.
As local societies expanded, they discovered that combining purchasing power could improve prices and strengthen supply networks. In 1863 independent cooperative societies established the Co-operative Wholesale Society (CWS) to supply the growing network of locally owned cooperative stores. Co-operative
The structure was effectively a cooperative whose participating organizations were themselves cooperatives—a practical form of federation.
It demonstrated an important possibility:
Scale can be achieved through relationships rather than consolidation.

The local cooperative could retain its identity.
Another cooperative could retain its identity.
Yet together they could create purchasing, manufacturing, distribution and organizational capabilities unavailable to either independently.
The arrangement was by no means perfect. Historical research describes significant tensions between the CWS and its member societies and characterizes aspects of the wider movement as a difficult federation. Yet the CWS nevertheless developed substantial manufacturing, commercial and organizational capabilities. Taylor & Francis Online
That imperfection is itself useful to Project Lichen.
Federation is not automatically resilient merely because it is decentralized.
Relationships must continually be maintained.

Cooperation Among Cooperatives
One of the modern cooperative principles expresses this explicitly:
Co-operation among co-operatives.

For Project Lichen, that principle can be generalized:
Co-operation among autonomous communities.

A community might be legally organized as a cooperative.
It might use a DAO.
It might be a seed library.
A watershed group.
A farm.
A foodshed network.
A communications cooperative.
A group of neighbours.
The organizational form is secondary to the underlying architecture:
Local autonomy + diversity + interoperability + voluntary cooperation = resilience.
From the Loom to the Network
There is an intriguing continuity between these histories and the technologies Project Lichen is now exploring.
The Lancashire mill worker developed ways of communicating when ordinary speech ceased to function.
The cooperative allowed people with little individual economic power to create collective economic capability.
Federations allowed small cooperatives to achieve capabilities normally associated with much larger organizations.
Today, systems such as decentralized communications, local computation and distributed governance explore the same general architectural possibility in another domain:
local capability without compulsory centralization.
Reticulum asks how independently operated nodes can communicate across many kinds of networks.
Freenet asks how information and applications can persist without depending upon one central server.
DAOScape asks how autonomous communities might coordinate governance.
The cooperative movement asks a much older question:
How can autonomous people accomplish together what they cannot accomplish separately?
Project Lichen does not need to choose between these traditions.
It can learn from all of them.
