# Seed Library

> **Food can fulfill today's need.  
> Seed can preserve the possibility of fulfilling tomorrow's.**

Seeds are among the simplest and most powerful examples of regenerative capability.

A viable seed can be food, genetic information, stored biological potential and the beginning of another generation.

Plant it successfully and one seed may become many.

Save some of those seeds and the productive capability can continue.

Share them and that capability can spread.

For Project Lichen, a seed library is therefore much more than a collection.

It is a **living store of capability**.

---

## From Seed Collection to Seed Stewardship

The seed work behind Project Lichen developed through years of growing, saving, exchanging and sharing seeds.

The Eugene Seed Exchange began formally in 2018 with seeds stored in a long kitchen drawer.

Over subsequent years the collection expanded through growing, exchanges, donations, seed events and relationships with other seed stewards.

By 2023 it contained 1,276 varieties and became the core of the Cascadia Seed Guild.

The collection has continued growing and now contains **more than 2,500 varieties**.

But the number itself is not the most important thing.

A seed sitting permanently in storage represents potential.

A seed that is planted, observed, reproduced, saved and shared becomes part of a living cycle.

> **A seed library becomes most valuable when its seeds continue living.**

---

## Seeds Are Not Static Objects

Seeds change.

They reproduce.

They encounter different soils, rainfall, temperatures, insects, diseases and growing practices.

They cross with other plants.

They are selected intentionally and unintentionally.

Some thrive.

Some fail.

Some gradually become better adapted to the places where they are repeatedly grown.

For this reason, Project Lichen is interested not only in preserving named varieties, but in observing what happens when seeds continue their relationship with a place.

The library therefore contains both **biological material and accumulated experience**.

---

## Diversity Preserves Possibility

There is no single perfect seed.

Different varieties carry different characteristics:

- flavor
- nutrition
- growth habit
- maturity time
- storage qualities
- drought tolerance
- cold tolerance
- disease response
- pollinator relationships
- soil interactions
- reproductive characteristics
- local adaptation

Diversity leaves options open.

Conditions that favor one variety this year may favor another in a future year.

A diverse seed library therefore preserves more than genetic diversity.

It preserves **possible responses to futures we cannot completely predict**.

---

## Edible Reproductive Material

Many seeds have another particularly interesting characteristic:

They are simultaneously **food and reproductive material**.

Beans, peas, grains, sunflower seeds and many other crops can potentially be:

**eaten**

**planted**

**stored**

**shared**

**exchanged**

One handful of beans therefore contains choices.

Eating them satisfies a present need.

Planting them may create food and many successor seeds.

Saving them preserves those choices for later.

Project Lichen describes these as **Edible Reproductive Materials (ERM)**.

## Seed Multiplication

One of the simplest observations we can make is:

> **How many viable seeds return from the seeds we plant?**

The answer varies enormously between species, varieties, seasons and growing conditions.

That variation is itself useful information.

Seed multiplication connects directly to regenerative capability because some of the harvest can be reserved for another planting while the remainder becomes available for food, storage or sharing.

The harvest is therefore not merely an endpoint.

It can also become the beginning of the next cycle.

**seed → plant → harvest → food + seed → next planting**

---

## Local Adaptation and Landraces

Repeated growing and seed saving can produce something particularly valuable: populations increasingly shaped by the places where they live.

Project Lichen is interested in locally adapted varieties and landraces not because they represent a final or perfect form, but because they demonstrate continuing relationships among:

**genetics ↔ environment ↔ selection ↔ time**

A seed population can carry evidence of previous seasons into future ones.

In this sense, seeds can become a form of biological memory.

---

## Sharing Preserves Capability

Seeds reproduce differently from most manufactured resources.

Under suitable conditions, giving seeds away need not permanently diminish the original supply.

The recipient may grow them, save them and eventually share even more.

This creates an unusual possibility:

> **Capability can increase through sharing.**

Seed exchanges, seed libraries and informal sharing networks can therefore distribute more than physical seeds.

They can distribute genetics, knowledge, experience and future productive possibility.

This principle has been central to the seed work from which Project Lichen emerged.

---

## A Living Library

Project Lichen's Seed Library will gradually document selected varieties, growing observations, seed histories, multiplication results, locally adapted populations and experiments in reproductive resilience.

It is not intended merely to become an online inventory.

The deeper questions are:

**What grows?**

**What reproduces?**

**What adapts?**

**What can be saved?**

**What can be shared?**

**What can future growers continue from here?**

And ultimately:

> **What capabilities remain?**

---

## Stewardship Across Generations

Every viable seed connects generations.

It exists because previous organisms successfully reproduced.

When we save it, we temporarily become its stewards.

When it is planted again, that stewardship passes forward.

A seed library can therefore be understood not simply as a collection of things from the past, but as a collection of **possibilities for the future**.

> **Save seed.  
> Grow seed.  
> Observe seed.  
> Share seed.  
> Keep possibility alive.**
