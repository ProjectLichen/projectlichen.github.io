# Recipes

> **If you eat, you're in.**

Food may be the most immediate connection most people have with living systems.

Every meal begins somewhere with soil, water, sunlight, microorganisms, plants, fungi, animals—or some combination of them.

Long before food reaches a kitchen it has already participated in an ecological story.

Recipes are where that story becomes part of everyday human life.

---

## Why Recipes Belong in Project Lichen

Project Lichen explores regenerative capability, seed stewardship, food production, biodiversity, technology and resilient communities.

Recipes might initially appear to be a different subject.

They are not.

Growing food without knowing how to use it leaves part of the cycle incomplete.

A productive garden becomes considerably more useful when its harvest can become nutritious, enjoyable food.

A seed variety becomes more valuable when people know how to grow it **and** want to eat what it produces.

Preserving culinary knowledge therefore helps preserve biological capability.

> **A crop that people enjoy eating has another reason to keep being grown.**

---

## From Seed to Meal to Seed

Many foods carry an unusual dual possibility.

A bean can become dinner.

The same bean can become next year's crop.

A squash can feed people while some of its seeds are retained for another generation.

A tomato can become sauce while its seeds continue a locally adapted line.

The cycle can therefore become:

**seed → plant → harvest → meal + saved seed → next planting**

The kitchen is not separate from seed stewardship.

It is one of the places where the value of that stewardship becomes tangible.

---

## Eating What Grows

Modern food systems can encourage us to begin with a recipe and then acquire whatever ingredients it requires.

A garden often reverses the question:

> **What is growing, and what can we make with it?**

Seasonal abundance invites creativity.

Too many tomatoes become sauce.

An unexpected quantity of beans becomes soups, stews, spreads and preserved foods.

Fruit ripening simultaneously invites drying, fermenting, preserving or sharing.

An unfamiliar crop invites experimentation.

Recipes can therefore help transform abundance into capability rather than waste.

---

## Preservation Extends the Harvest

Harvest and consumption do not need to occur at the same moment.

Drying, fermenting, freezing, pickling, canning and other preservation methods allow food produced during abundance to remain available later.

Preservation changes the temporal distance between production and need.

It can also reduce dependence upon distant food systems during seasons when local production is limited.

Project Lichen is interested in preservation not merely as storage, but as another form of retained capability.

---

## Food Knowledge Is a Capability

Knowing that a plant is edible is useful.

Knowing how to grow it is another capability.

Knowing when to harvest it adds another.

Knowing how to prepare, preserve and enjoy it adds still more.

This knowledge can be lost.

Recipes therefore preserve more than ingredient lists.

They can preserve:

**techniques**

**seasonal knowledge**

**cultural memory**

**ways of using abundance**

**methods of preservation**

**relationships between crops**

**reasons for continuing to grow particular varieties**

Like seeds, culinary knowledge becomes more resilient when it is shared.

---

## Recipes as Experiments

Project Lichen recipes need not always be fixed instructions.

Gardens vary.

Harvests vary.

Ingredients vary.

Tastes vary.

Sometimes the question is simply:

> **What can we make from what we have?**

A recipe may therefore record substitutions, experiments, preservation methods and unexpected combinations alongside conventional measurements and instructions.

Successful experiments can be repeated.

Interesting failures can teach us something.

And occasionally an accidental combination becomes a tradition.

---

## Reducing Food Waste

Food capability includes making useful decisions about abundance.

Surplus food might be:

**eaten**

**preserved**

**shared**

**fed appropriately to other organisms**

**fermented**

**composted**

**returned to soil**

The objective is not necessarily to eliminate every residue.

Living systems continually produce residues.

The more interesting question is:

> **Can those materials remain within useful cycles?**

Food that cannot feed people may still feed another part of the living system.

---

## From Garden to Table and Back Again

Recipes reveal something important about Project Lichen.

Living systems are not something happening outside the human world.

We participate in them.

When we select seed, grow plants, harvest food, cook meals, save seed, compost residues and return biological material to soil, we participate in a cycle:

**soil → seed → plant → food → people → organic material → soil**

There is no obvious point at which nature ends and human activity begins.

There are relationships.

## An Evolving Collection

This section will gradually contain recipes and food experiments arising from seasonal harvests, donated produce, seed crops and everyday cooking.

Some may be carefully measured.

Others may begin with:

> **We had a lot of this. What could we do with it?**

Both belong here.

Because ultimately the purpose of a regenerative food system is not simply to produce impressive plants.

It is to participate in sustaining life.
