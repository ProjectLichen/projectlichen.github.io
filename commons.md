# The Commons

> **Some capabilities become stronger when they are shared.**

A seed can be planted and multiplied.

Knowledge can be taught without removing it from the person who already knows it.

Software can be copied, studied and adapted.

A recipe can travel between generations.

An observation made in one place may help someone understand what they are seeing somewhere else.

These are unusual forms of value.

Sharing them can increase the capability of the wider system.

Project Lichen uses **The Commons** to explore that possibility.

---

## Beyond Ownership

Many human systems are organized around ownership.

Who possesses the land?

Who owns the object?

Who controls the resource?

Who has the right to use it?

These are important questions, but they do not completely describe a commons.

Project Lichen is interested in another question:

> **What are we collectively keeping capable?**

A seed commons must remain capable of reproduction.

A knowledge commons must remain capable of being understood and transmitted.

A software commons must remain capable of being inspected, maintained and adapted.

A biological commons must retain the relationships that allow living systems to continue.

The existence of a resource does not necessarily mean its capability has been preserved.

---

## Sharing Can Multiply Capability

Many physical resources become scarcer when divided.

If ten people divide ten apples, each receives fewer apples.

But some capabilities behave differently.

Give someone knowledge and the original knowledge need not disappear.

Share viable seed and both participants may eventually possess more seed.

Publish open software and another person may improve it without preventing the original author from continuing to use it.

Share a successful growing technique and it may be tested, adapted and shared again.

This suggests an important distinction:

> **Transfer moves something.  
> Sharing can sometimes multiply capability.**

---

## A Commons Requires Stewardship

A commons does not sustain itself simply because everyone has access to it.

Seeds can lose viability.

Soil can be degraded.

Water can be polluted.

Software can become unmaintained.

Knowledge can lose context.

Shared resources can be exhausted.

A functioning commons therefore requires stewardship.

The relevant question is not merely:

**Can people access this?**

but also:

> **What relationships and responsibilities allow this capability to continue?**

---

## Seeds as a Commons

Seeds provide one of Project Lichen's clearest examples.

A viable seed contains reproductive possibility.

If all of a harvest is consumed, that immediate food value may be realized while reproductive capability disappears.

If some seed is retained, another choice remains available.

If some is shared, that reproductive possibility can become distributed.

The result is not simply a larger inventory of seeds.

It can become a network of growers, environments, observations, selections and locally evolving populations.

The commons exists partly in the seed and partly in the relationships surrounding it.

---

## Knowledge as a Commons

Knowledge behaves similarly.

An observation becomes more useful when enough context is preserved for someone else to understand it.

This is one reason Project Lichen records not merely conclusions but questions, experiments, failures and changing interpretations.

Knowledge without provenance can lose capability.

A statement tells us something.

Knowing **where it came from, what was observed and under what conditions** may allow us to do something with it.

The [Field Notes](field-notes.md) and [Research Library](research.md) are therefore parts of the Project Lichen commons.

---

## Technology as a Commons

Open technology creates another interesting form of shared capability.

Software that can be inspected can be understood.

Software that can be modified can be adapted.

Documentation allows knowledge to move beyond its original creators.

Open standards can allow independently created systems to cooperate.

None of these guarantees resilience.

But they preserve possibilities.

Project Lichen therefore has a natural interest in open-source software, open documentation and technologies whose operation can remain understandable to the people who depend upon them.

See [Technology](technology.md).

---

## Food Knowledge as a Commons

Growing food is only part of food capability.

People also need to know:

**when to harvest it**

**how to prepare it**

**how to preserve it**

**how to store it**

**how to save reproductive material**

**how to grow it again**

Recipes, preservation techniques and growing knowledge therefore belong to the commons too.

A crop that nobody remembers how to use has lost part of its relationship with people.

See [Recipes](recipes.md).

---

## Diversity Strengthens the Commons

A resilient commons need not require everyone to possess the same thing or know the same thing.

Its strength may come from distribution.

One person may preserve a seed variety.

Another may know how to grow it.

Another may know how to cook it.

Another may understand its history.

Another may discover that it performs particularly well under different conditions.

Together they possess capabilities that none necessarily possesses alone.

> **Resilience can reside in relationships.**

---

## The Commons Across Generations

Some of the people who may benefit from what we preserve today have not yet been born.

This changes the idea of sharing.

Stewardship is not only horizontal—among people alive now.

It is also longitudinal.

Seeds, knowledge, soil, biodiversity, functioning ecosystems, technologies and cultural practices can move through generations.

We temporarily participate in their histories.

This is one reason Project Lichen describes its work as:

> **Stewardship Across Generations**

---

## The Commons Is Not Free of Responsibility

Calling something a commons does not mean that everything must be available without limits.

Living systems have boundaries.

Communities have responsibilities.

Resources can be damaged by unrestricted use.

Knowledge may require context and care.

The challenge is therefore not simply to maximize access.

It is to develop forms of participation that allow shared capabilities to remain viable.

That is a continuing inquiry rather than a solved problem.

---

## What Capabilities Are We Keeping Alive?

Project Lichen approaches The Commons through questions:

**What capability is being shared?**

**Can it reproduce?**

**What does it depend upon?**

**Who can participate?**

**What knowledge is required?**

**What responsibilities accompany access?**

**Can the capability adapt?**

**Can it survive the loss of any one participant?**

**Can it pass to another generation?**

And ultimately:

> **Does sharing leave the wider system more capable?**

---

> **A commons is not merely something we share.  
> It is something we collectively keep capable.**
