# Technology

Technology is part of the living world because the organisms creating and using it are part of the living world.

The useful question for Project Lichen is therefore not simply:

> **Is technology good or bad?**

but:

> **What capabilities does a technology preserve, enlarge, distribute, concentrate—or extinguish?**

That question allows technology to be examined alongside soil, seed, water, energy, knowledge and human coordination rather than treated as something separate from them.

---

## Technology as Capability

A tool extends capability.

A shovel changes what a person can do with soil.

A water tank changes when captured rainfall can be used.

A greenhouse changes the conditions under which plants can grow.

A computer changes how information can be processed, preserved and shared.

A network changes how people and machines can coordinate.

Artificial intelligence changes what kinds of patterns can be recognized, interpreted and explored.

Each technology creates possibilities.

Each may also create dependencies.

Project Lichen is interested in both.

---

## The Capability Behind the Tool

Possessing a technology is not necessarily the same as possessing the capability it appears to provide.

A machine may depend upon:

**energy**

**materials**

**replacement parts**

**software**

**networks**

**specialized knowledge**

**distant infrastructure**

**economic systems**

A technology can therefore increase immediate capability while simultaneously increasing dependency elsewhere.

This does not make the technology undesirable.

It makes the relationships worth understanding.

> **What must continue functioning for this capability to remain available?**

---

## Appropriate Does Not Mean Primitive

Project Lichen does not assume that smaller, older or simpler technology is inherently better.

Nor does it assume that newer, larger or more computationally powerful technology is necessarily progress.

The appropriate scale depends upon the problem.

Sometimes a hand tool is sufficient.

Sometimes a sensor can reveal something that human observation cannot easily detect.

Sometimes computation near the source of data can avoid moving enormous quantities of information elsewhere.

Sometimes large shared infrastructure provides capabilities that would be impractical to reproduce locally.

The question remains:

> **What are we trying to enable, and what does enabling it require?**

---

## Data Distance

Digital systems introduce another form of distance.

Information may travel through sensors, devices, networks, protocols, servers, data centres and software layers before useful meaning is extracted from it.

Project Lichen uses **Data Distance** as an evolving way of examining that journey.

Distance need not be purely geographical.

It may also be:

**physical**

**network**

**computational**

**protocol**

**architectural**

**representational**

The question becomes:

> **How far must data travel before meaning can be extracted and useful action becomes possible?**

Reducing unnecessary data movement may reduce energy use, infrastructure requirements, latency and dependency.

Sometimes the most useful computation may therefore occur close to where the information originates.

---

## Local and Distributed Capability

Project Lichen is particularly interested in technologies that allow useful capability to exist at multiple scales.

A sensor might interpret a change where it occurs rather than continuously transmitting raw measurements.

A local computer might perform useful inference without depending upon a remote data centre.

Several modest machines might cooperate rather than requiring one increasingly large centralized system.

Information might be summarized before traveling farther.

These possibilities raise an important question:

> **How much capability can remain near the place where it is needed?**

This does not require rejecting centralized systems.

It gives us another dimension by which to evaluate them.

---

## Technology and Living Systems

Living systems provide useful contrasts to many engineered systems.

They are distributed.

They operate through enormous numbers of local interactions.

They recycle materials.

They adapt.

They tolerate variation.

They store information in many forms.

They frequently achieve complex outcomes without centralized control.

This does not mean computers should simply imitate biology.

But living systems may reveal alternative ways of thinking about computation, networks, resilience and coordination.

Likewise, technology may give us new ways of observing living systems.

The exchange can work in both directions.

---

## E-Cropolis

[E-Cropolis](e-cropolis.md) provides one experimental environment for exploring these relationships.

Rather than attempting to prescribe the ideal sustainable city, E-Cropolis asks whether a simulated city can progressively recover living capabilities.

Technology, energy, food, water, soil, seed, biodiversity, materials, economics and human activity can then be explored as interacting systems rather than isolated categories.

The simulation determines what happens.

Artificial intelligence may help us understand what happened and suggest what might be tried.

Humans decide what matters and what to do next.

---

## Open Systems and Shared Knowledge

Project Lichen has a natural affinity with open systems.

When software, observations, methods and knowledge can be inspected and shared, other people gain the possibility of:

**understanding them**

**questioning them**

**repairing them**

**adapting them**

**building upon them**

This does not guarantee good outcomes.

But it preserves possibilities that disappear when knowledge becomes inaccessible.

In that sense, open knowledge has something interesting in common with seed.

> **Sharing can increase the capability of the wider system.**

---

## Technology as an Inquiry

Project Lichen does not propose a final technological architecture.

Instead we ask:

**What does this technology enable?**

**What does it require?**

**Where does its energy come from?**

**How far must its data travel?**

**What dependencies does it create?**

**Who can understand or repair it?**

**Can its knowledge be shared?**

**What happens when part of the supporting system fails?**

And ultimately:

> **What capabilities remain?**

---

> **Technology should not merely help us do more.  
> It should help us understand what remains possible afterward.**
