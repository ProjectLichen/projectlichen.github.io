# Roadmap

> **We know the direction of inquiry without assuming that we already know the destination.**

Project Lichen is an evolving framework rather than a finished system.

Some parts already exist in practice.

Some are being documented and connected.

Some are active experiments.

Others are possibilities that have emerged from those experiments but have not yet been tested.

This roadmap records that landscape.

It is deliberately not a schedule.

---

## Already Growing

These are not proposals.

They are practical activities, observations and bodies of knowledge from which Project Lichen has emerged.

### Seed Stewardship

Years of growing, saving, exchanging and sharing seeds have developed into a living seed collection containing more than 2,500 varieties.

The continuing work includes:

- growing and reproducing seed
- maintaining diversity
- observing local adaptation
- developing landraces
- recording harvest and multiplication
- sharing seed and knowledge
- connecting growers and seed stewards

See [Seed Library](seed-library.md).

### Living Systems

Practical work continues with soils, food production, pollinators, biodiversity, fungi, water, perennial systems and biological material cycles.

Observation across seasons provides much of the empirical foundation for Project Lichen.

See [Living Systems](living-systems.md) and [Field Notes](field-notes.md).

### Food and Culinary Knowledge

Growing food is only useful when people can use what is produced.

Recipes, preservation methods and practical knowledge help connect seed stewardship and food production with everyday life.

See [Recipes](recipes.md).

### Research

Research from many disciplines is continually examined for relationships with Project Lichen questions and practical observations.

See [Research Library](research.md).

## Being Developed

These are ideas and methods that have emerged from practical work and are now being made more explicit.

### Regenerative Capability

Project Lichen is exploring ways of understanding systems through the capabilities they retain rather than only through what they possess or produce.

The central question is:

> **What capabilities remain?**

See [Regenerative Capability](regenerative-capability.md).

### Reproductive Resilience

Seeds and other reproductive materials provide an opportunity to examine resilience through measurable biological multiplication.

The experimental **Reproductive Resilience Index (RRI)** explores characteristics such as:

- reproductive multiplication
- nutritional usefulness
- storage
- propagation requirements
- external inputs
- adaptability
- local adaptation
- maturity time
- ecological functions
- sharing and exchange

RRI remains an evolving inquiry rather than a finished metric.

### Edible Reproductive Materials

Some foods are simultaneously consumable resources and reproductive materials.

Beans, peas, grains, seeds, tubers and other crops can preserve choices between:

**eat → store → plant → share → exchange**

Project Lichen is exploring these as **Edible Reproductive Materials (ERM)** and as stores of future capability.

### Alignment With Life

Project Lichen is examining whether objectives can be evaluated not only by whether they were achieved, but by what possibilities remain afterward.

> **Alignment should not be measured solely by whether an objective was achieved, but by what capabilities remain—and what possibilities have been enlarged or extinguished—after it was achieved.**

See [Alignment With Life](alignment-with-life.md).

## Active Technological Inquiry

Technology is increasingly important to Project Lichen because information, computation, sensing, simulation and communication affect what can be observed and acted upon.

### Data Distance

Data Distance asks:

> **How far must data travel before meaning can be extracted and useful action becomes possible?**

The inquiry includes physical, network, computational, protocol, architectural and representational distance.

Current interests include:

- local computation
- edge computing
- distributed systems
- computation near sensors
- reducing unnecessary data movement
- local artificial intelligence
- low-energy computing
- extracting meaning before transmitting raw information

See [Technology](technology.md).

### Local Experimental Computing

Small local systems provide opportunities to test ideas about distributed capability rather than treating them only as architectural concepts.

Experiments may explore combinations of:

- conventional computers
- Linux systems
- mobile devices
- local AI models
- sensors
- network devices
- small embedded computers
- distributed agents
- DAO-based governance and coordination
- points and token-based economic systems

The objective is not simply to build a larger collection of computers.

It is to investigate where useful computation and meaning extraction can occur.

## E-Cropolis

E-Cropolis is an emerging Project Lichen experiment built upon the lineage of SimCity and its open-source descendant Micropolis.

Its starting question is:

> **How much living capability can an existing city recover without ceasing to be a city?**

Rather than replacing the city with an idealized ecological settlement, E-Cropolis explores whether an existing urban system can progressively acquire or recover capabilities involving:

**food**

**soil**

**water**

**seed**

**energy**

**biodiversity**

**materials and cycles**

**knowledge**

**community**

The underlying simulation should determine what happens.

Artificial intelligence may help observe patterns, interpret results, form hypotheses and propose experiments.

Humans decide what matters and what to try next.

> **Don't simulate the answer.  
> Simulate the relationships from which answers can emerge.**

See [E-Cropolis](e-cropolis.md).

## The Commons

Project Lichen is exploring the possibility that some forms of capability become stronger when shared.

Seeds can reproduce.

Knowledge can spread without disappearing from its source.

Open software can be copied and adapted.

Observations can acquire additional meaning when compared with observations made elsewhere.

This suggests a commons based not merely upon shared possession but upon shared stewardship:

> **A commons is not merely something we share.  
> It is something we collectively keep capable.**

See [The Commons](commons.md).

## Emerging Possibilities

The following directions have emerged naturally from the work but remain exploratory.

### Capability Mapping

Can we describe the capabilities present within a garden, farm, neighborhood, city or community?

Instead of asking only what resources exist, we might ask:

**What can this place currently do?**

**What can reproduce?**

**What can be repaired locally?**

**What knowledge exists?**

**What depends upon distant systems?**

**Which capabilities are fragile?**

**Which capabilities could multiply?**

### Capability Across Scales

Do similar principles appear at different scales?

For example:

**seed → garden → farm → foodshed → city → region**

or:

**sensor → device → local network → data centre → global network**

Understanding where capability resides—and where dependency enters—may reveal relationships that conventional inventories miss.

### Material Distance

Just as information has Data Distance, physical resources have journeys.

Food, nutrients, packaging, construction materials, waste and manufactured goods move through systems before and after use.

Could understanding those journeys reveal opportunities to shorten cycles and retain useful material capability locally?

### Living-System Simulation

E-Cropolis may eventually allow different interventions to be tested across simulated decades.

Different strategies could begin from identical conditions and diverge over time.

The interesting question would not necessarily be:

**Which produced the highest score?**

but:

> **What remained possible in each future?**

## Questions We Do Not Yet Know How to Answer

Some questions should remain open.

How should regenerative capability be measured without turning it into another target that distorts the system?

How much capability should remain local and how much is better shared across larger networks?

When does efficiency increase resilience, and when does it remove redundancy that resilience requires?

How do we value biological capability without simply financializing it?

How do we compare capabilities that cannot meaningfully be reduced to the same unit?

How do human communities coordinate stewardship across generations?

How can artificial intelligence help us understand complex systems without becoming the authority that decides what those systems should become?

How do we recognize capabilities that we do not yet know are important?

These are not deficiencies in the roadmap.

They are part of the roadmap.

---

## How This Roadmap Changes

Project Lichen will change as observations accumulate.

Some ideas will become experiments.

Some experiments will become useful methods.

Some will fail.

Some questions may turn out to have been poorly framed.

New relationships will appear.

When evidence contradicts an assumption, the assumption should change.

The roadmap therefore records **where the inquiry is going**, not where it is required to end.

---

## The Direction

Across seeds, soil, food, water, technology, cities, knowledge and communities, Project Lichen continues to return to a small set of questions:

**What can this system do?**

**What does that capability depend upon?**

**Can it reproduce or regenerate?**

**Can it adapt?**

**Can it be shared?**

**What happens when conditions change?**

And ultimately:

> **What capabilities remain?**
